## Research Question

Mathematics requires repeated practice, but repetition can feel discouraging before a learner develops confidence. I wanted to investigate whether a quiz with selectable grade levels, scoring, and immediate solutions could make practice feel more interesting.

## Application Design

The application was designed for learners from elementary through high school. Users begin at an index screen, choose a grade level, answer a set of questions, and then receive the correct answer and an explanation.

The original plan included ten elementary, five middle-school, and two high-school questions. Entry's scene limit required a smaller prototype: three elementary questions, two middle-school questions, and one high-school question. This constraint changed the scope but allowed the full interaction to be completed.

## User Observation

Seven people tried the prototype: five fourth-grade classmates, one middle-school student, and one adult family member.

- The fourth-grade students answered the elementary questions but found the middle-school level difficult.
- The middle-school student answered through the middle-school level but could not complete the high-school question.
- The adult participant completed every level and scored 100.
- Participants generally described the quiz as enjoyable, but several wanted a larger question set.

These observations suggested that the level divisions were broadly understandable and that the interaction could create short-term interest. However, the small and informal sample did not establish that the application increased study time or improved mathematics scores.

## What I Learned

This was my first independent research report. Choosing a question before building the software was more difficult than beginning with a sudden invention idea, but it helped me think about the difference between making an application and testing a claim about learning.

## Limitations and Next Steps

The prototype contained too few questions to support repeated practice. Participant satisfaction was observed informally, without a consistent questionnaire, pre-test, post-test, or usage data. A stronger study would include more questions, adaptive difficulty, repeated sessions, and measurable outcomes such as time spent practicing or change in performance.

The next version could select new questions based on each learner's results and provide a more systematic way to study the relationship between engagement and learning.
